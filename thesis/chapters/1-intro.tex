\chapter{Introduction}

The increasing use of small unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) creates new challenges for aviation safety, especially in low-altitude airspace where helicopters frequently operate. Helicopters are commonly used for rescue missions, police operations, infrastructure inspection, medical transport, and low-altitude transport tasks. In such environments, non-cooperative drones can become safety-critical obstacles because they may not carry transponders, may not participate in cooperative traffic management systems, and may be difficult to detect visually under adverse lighting, cluttered backgrounds, or high pilot workload. Detect-and-avoid (DAA) systems therefore require robust onboard perception methods that can detect, track, and predict the motion of such intruders early enough to support collision avoidance decisions \cite{fasano2016senseAvoid,skowron2019senseAvoidSmallUAS,riedel2025daaStandards}.

While DAA has been studied extensively for unmanned aircraft systems, the problem of non-cooperative drone detection from a helicopter perspective remains challenging. Classical cooperative aviation systems rely on communication or transponder-based information, but small consumer or commercial drones may not provide such data. Optical sensors can offer high angular resolution, but they suffer from illumination, weather, and background-dependence. Radar can provide long-range detection, but small drones often have weak and variable radar signatures. In Contrast, LiDAR sensors provide direct three-dimensional range measurements and deliver object positions without requiring visual texture or object cooperation. This makes LiDAR an attractive sensor modality for detecting small airborne objects in the vicinity of a helicopter.

However, LiDAR-based drone detection is difficult for several reasons. Small drones occupy only a small physical volume and may generate very sparse point returns, especially at longer ranges. Additionally, unlike cars or pedestrians in autonomous driving, drones are not constrained to a ground plane. Their altitude is a central part of their state, and reliable vertical localization is essential. Finally, the perception of an intruder drones position alone is insufficient, a helicopter DAA system needs not only a real-time per-frame detection, but also an estimate of the intruder state, including position, velocity, and short-horizon trajectory.

This dissertation project addresses the problem of tracking and trajectory prediction of non-cooperative drones from sparse airborne LiDAR observations. The central motivation is that a single LiDAR frame may contain too little information for robust decision making, whereas temporal integration can improve state estimation, bridge short detection gaps, and enable prediction of future intruder positions. The research therefore connects three technical areas: LiDAR-based 3D object detection, multi-object tracking, and short-horizon trajectory prediction.

Modern 3D object detection methods such as PointPillars, SECOND, PV-RCNN, CenterPoint, and PillarNet have shown strong performance on autonomous-driving point-cloud benchmarks \cite{lang2019pointpillars,yan2018second,shi2020pvrcnn,yin2021centerpoint,shi2022pillarnet}. Nevertheless, these methods are mostly developed for ground-based road traffic scenes, where objects are relatively large and altitude is strongly constrained by the road surface. In contrast, drones are small, airborne, and may move freely in three dimensions. This creates a gap between existing LiDAR perception methods and the requirements of helicopter-oriented DAA.

The planned research therefore investigates how sparse LiDAR measurements can be converted into robust tracks and future trajectory estimates for non-cooperative drones. The expected contribution is a perception pipeline that goes beyond detecting drone bounding boxes and instead produces safety-relevant intruder state estimates and short-term motion predictions.
