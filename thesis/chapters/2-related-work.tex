\chapter{State of the Art}

Detect-and-avoid systems aim to enable aircraft or unmanned systems to detect potential conflicts and perform avoidance actions without relying solely on human visual observation. The broader DAA literature distinguishes between cooperative systems, where other traffic broadcasts its state, and non-cooperative systems, where the intruder must be sensed directly. Surveys on sense-and-avoid and UAS collision avoidance highlight that reliable perception, tracking, and conflict assessment remain central challenges for integrating unmanned aircraft into shared airspace \cite{fasano2016senseAvoid,skowron2019senseAvoidSmallUAS,pham2015collisionAvoidanceSurvey}. For helicopter operations, the problem is especially relevant because helicopters often fly at low altitude and may operate outside controlled airport environments. 

\section{Drone Detection and the Role of LiDAR}

To that end drone detection has been studied using multiple sensor modalities, including cameras, radar, radio-frequency sensing, acoustic sensing, thermal imaging, and LiDAR. Recent surveys show that each sensor modality has characteristic strengths and limitations \cite{seidaliyeva2024advancesChallenges,rahman2024uavDetectionSurvey}. Camera-based systems are mature and benefit from image-based neural networks, but their performance depends heavily on illumination, weather, contrast, and background appearance. Radar can provide range and velocity estimates, but small drones may have weak radar cross sections, which makes precise localization difficult. LiDAR provides direct geometric range measurements and is therefore attractive for estimating object position in three-dimensional space \cite{seidaliyeva2025lidarUAVDetection}.

LiDAR-based detection of small UAVs has been investigated in several works. Hammer et al.\ studied the potential of LiDAR sensors for UAV detection and later explored detection, tracking, and classification with 360-degree LiDAR systems and sensor fusion \cite{hammer2018potentialLidarUAV,hammer2018lidarSmallUAVs,hammer2019sensorFusion360Lidar}. Dogru and Marques specifically address drone detection from sparse LiDAR measurements, highlighting the challenge that small aerial objects may generate only a limited number of points \cite{dogru2022sparseLidarDrone}. More recent work investigates real-time drone detection with airborne LiDAR and deep learning, including synthetic-data-based training and tracking-by-detection pipelines \cite{manduhu2023airborneSenseDetect}. These works demonstrate the feasibility of LiDAR-based UAV detection, but they also show that sparsity, range, sensor geometry, and temporal stability remain open issues.

A central limitation in much of the existing work is that detection is often treated as the primary output. For a safety-oriented helicopter application, however, a detector must be embedded into a temporal perception system. A single-frame bounding box does not directly answer whether a drone is approaching, whether it will intersect the helicopter path, or how reliable the estimate is. Therefore, tracking and trajectory prediction are necessary extensions of LiDAR-based drone detection.

\section{3D LiDAR Object Detection}

Deep 3D object detection from point clouds has advanced rapidly in autonomous driving. Early point-based architectures such as PointNet and PointNet++ showed that neural networks can operate directly on unordered point sets \cite{qi2017pointnet,qi2017pointnetpp}. Voxel-based methods such as VoxelNet and SECOND convert point clouds into structured grids and apply sparse or dense convolutional networks \cite{zhou2018voxelnet,yan2018second}. PointPillars introduced a particularly efficient representation by grouping points into vertical pillars and producing a bird's-eye-view pseudo-image for fast 2D convolutional detection \cite{lang2019pointpillars}. Later methods such as PV-RCNN combine voxel and point representations, while CenterPoint formulates 3D detection as center-based object localization and tracking \cite{shi2020pvrcnn,yin2021centerpoint}. PillarNet further improves pillar-based detection performance while maintaining real-time efficiency \cite{shi2022pillarnet}.

These architectures are commonly evaluated on large autonomous-driving datasets such as KITTI, nuScenes, and Waymo Open Dataset \cite{geiger2012kitti,caesar2020nuscenes,sun2020waymo}. Such datasets have driven major progress, but their object classes and scene assumptions differ substantially from airborne drone detection. Cars, pedestrians, and cyclists are usually located on or near a ground plane. Their vertical position is therefore highly constrained. In contrast, drones are small airborne objects with free three-dimensional motion. This difference is important because many efficient LiDAR detectors rely heavily on bird's-eye-view representations. They can localize objects accurately in the horizontal plane, but their vertical localization may be less strongly constrained by the architecture and training objective.

For the proposed research, this creates both an opportunity and a challenge. Existing 3D detectors can be adapted to detect drones in LiDAR point clouds, but their outputs must be evaluated not only with generic detection metrics, but also with respect to the quality of temporal state estimation. In particular, errors in height, velocity, and track continuity may be more important for drone DAA than average precision alone.

\section{Tracking-by-Detection and State Estimation}

Tracking-by-detection is a widely used paradigm in which an object detector provides candidate detections in each frame and a tracking algorithm associates detections over time. Classical multi-object tracking relies on prediction, association, and update steps. The Kalman filter provides a foundational method for recursively estimating the state of a dynamic system from noisy measurements \cite{kalman1960newApproach}. In the drone tracking context, a simple state may include three-dimensional position and velocity. The filter predicts the next state using a motion model, compares this prediction with a new detection, and corrects the state estimate according to the assumed measurement uncertainty.

Data association is another central component. The Hungarian algorithm provides a classical solution for assigning detections to tracks based on a cost matrix \cite{kuhn1955hungarian}. Modern tracking-by-detection systems often combine motion prediction with assignment. SORT demonstrated that a simple combination of Kalman filtering and Hungarian assignment can achieve efficient online tracking in image sequences \cite{bewley2016sort}. DeepSORT extends this idea with appearance-based association \cite{wojke2017deepsort}. For 3D object tracking, AB3DMOT provides a strong and simple baseline for LiDAR-based multi-object tracking and highlights the importance of appropriate 3D tracking metrics \cite{weng2020ab3dmot}.

For sparse airborne LiDAR drone tracking, these ideas are particularly relevant because detections may be intermittent and noisy. A tracker can bridge short gaps, smooth noisy detections, and estimate velocity. However, standard tracking assumptions may be challenged by small object size, sparse point returns, and fast three-dimensional motion. Moreover, many tracking methods are developed for road traffic, where object motion is constrained by lanes and the ground plane. Non-cooperative drones may accelerate, change altitude, hover, or cross paths in ways that require adapted motion models and uncertainty handling.

\section{Trajectory Prediction}

Trajectory prediction aims to estimate future object states from past observations. In autonomous driving and robotics, prediction is often formulated as a sequence forecasting problem, where the model receives a history of positions and predicts one or multiple plausible future trajectories. Classical baselines include constant-velocity and constant-acceleration models, often implemented through Kalman or interacting multiple model filters \cite{mazor1998immSurvey}. Learning-based approaches use recurrent networks, graph models, or transformer-like architectures to capture more complex motion patterns and interactions \cite{alahi2016socialLSTM,salzmann2020trajectronpp}.

For UAVs, trajectory prediction has been studied in both cooperative and non-cooperative settings. Xie and Chen propose an efficient online trajectory prediction method for non-cooperative UAVs, directly aligning with the need to predict future motion without relying on transmitted intent information \cite{xie2022noncooperativeUAVPrediction}. Other work investigates deep learning methods for flying vehicle trajectory prediction and flight-state-aware UAV trajectory forecasting \cite{tan2023flyingVehicleTrajectory,zhang2024flightStateRecognition}. These methods indicate that UAV trajectory prediction is an active field, but the combination with sparse LiDAR perception remains insufficiently explored.

In the proposed dissertation topic, trajectory prediction is not treated as an isolated forecasting problem. Instead, it is linked to perception uncertainty. The predictor receives tracks that originate from sparse LiDAR detections and therefore contain errors, missed detections, and uncertain state estimates. This makes the problem different from prediction using clean ground-truth trajectories. A key research question is therefore how tracking uncertainty, detection confidence, and sparse point-cloud observations can be incorporated into short-horizon drone trajectory prediction.

\section{Synthetic Data and Benchmarking}

A practical challenge in LiDAR-based drone perception is the scarcity of suitable datasets. Large public 3D detection datasets focus mostly on autonomous driving, while datasets for small airborne drone detection with LiDAR are limited. Synthetic data can help address this problem by enabling controlled variation of sensor properties, drone geometry, trajectories, ranges, and environmental conditions. However, synthetic data introduces a simulation-to-reality gap. Dieter et al.\ study this gap for deep-learning-based drone detection and show that synthetic data must be carefully designed and validated to support real-world generalization \cite{dieter2023simulationRealityGap}. Recent airborne LiDAR work also uses simulation and digital-twin concepts to generate training data for drone detection \cite{manduhu2023airborneSenseDetect}.

For this dissertation, synthetic data is relevant in two ways. First, it enables systematic generation of sparse LiDAR drone encounters, including rare or safety-critical scenarios that are difficult to collect experimentally. Second, it allows controlled evaluation of tracking and trajectory prediction methods under known ground truth. Nevertheless, the resulting methods must be assessed with respect to generalization. A benchmark should therefore separate training and unseen test scenarios and should report not only detection accuracy, but also tracking continuity, velocity estimation, future position error, and conflict-relevant quantities.

\section{Research Gap}

The reviewed literature shows substantial progress in DAA systems, LiDAR-based 3D detection, multi-object tracking, and trajectory prediction. However, the specific problem of tracking and predicting non-cooperative drones from sparse airborne LiDAR remains insufficiently addressed. Existing 3D detectors are largely optimized for ground-based autonomous-driving scenarios. Existing drone detection work often focuses on per-frame detection or ground-based counter-UAV sensing. Existing trajectory prediction methods frequently assume cleaner trajectory observations than are available from sparse LiDAR detections.

The central research gap is therefore the lack of an integrated, safety-oriented pipeline that converts sparse airborne LiDAR measurements of small non-cooperative drones into temporally stable tracks and short-horizon trajectory predictions. Addressing this gap requires combining LiDAR-based detection, uncertainty-aware tracking, and trajectory forecasting in a way that is suitable for helicopter detect-and-avoid scenarios. The proposed dissertation will investigate this connection and evaluate how temporal integration can improve the reliability of drone state estimation beyond single-frame detection.
