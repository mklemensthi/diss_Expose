\chapter{Research Question}

\section{Motivation}

The previous chapters have shown that LiDAR-based drone perception is positioned at the intersection of detect-and-avoid, 3D object detection, multi-object tracking, and trajectory prediction. While each of these areas has developed substantially, the specific problem of tracking and predicting the motion of small non-cooperative drones from sparse airborne LiDAR measurements remains insufficiently addressed. Existing detect-and-avoid research emphasizes the need for reliable perception and conflict awareness, but often treats sensing, tracking, and avoidance at a system level \cite{fasano2016senseAvoid,skowron2019senseAvoidSmallUAS,riedel2025daaStandards}. Existing 3D LiDAR object detection methods, in contrast, have largely been developed for autonomous driving, where objects are typically constrained to the ground plane \cite{lang2019pointpillars,yan2018second,shi2020pvrcnn,yin2021centerpoint,shi2022pillarnet}.

For a helicopter-oriented detect-and-avoid application, however, the perception problem differs from road-scene perception in several important ways. A drone is small, airborne, and not bound to a road surface. Its vertical position and vertical motion are therefore central parts of its state. Moreover, at safety-relevant distances, a small drone may produce only a small number of LiDAR points, making single-frame detection uncertain or intermittent \cite{dogru2022sparseLidarDrone,seidaliyeva2025lidarUAVDetection}. A system that only reports independent 3D bounding boxes per frame is therefore insufficient. It must also establish whether detections belong to the same physical object over time, estimate position and velocity, bridge short detection gaps, and predict future motion over a short horizon.

The central premise of this dissertation is that temporal integration is essential for sparse LiDAR-based drone perception. Tracking can reduce the effect of noisy detections, estimate velocity, and provide object identity over time. Trajectory prediction can then use these tracks to estimate future drone positions, which are necessary for downstream conflict assessment. The research gap is therefore not merely the detection of drones in individual LiDAR frames, but the conversion of sparse and uncertain LiDAR detections into temporally stable and prediction-capable intruder state estimates.

\section{Main Research Question}

The main research question of this dissertation is:

\begin{quote}
\textit{How can sparse airborne LiDAR observations of small non-cooperative drones be transformed into robust object tracks and short-horizon trajectory predictions suitable for helicopter detect-and-avoid perception?}
\end{quote}

This question contains three connected aspects. First, the LiDAR measurements must provide a sufficiently realistic and challenging basis for studying small airborne targets. Second, per-frame detections must be associated and filtered over time to form stable tracks. Third, these tracks must be used to estimate future drone motion over a short time horizon. The dissertation therefore investigates the complete perception chain from sparse LiDAR point clouds to temporally consistent drone state estimation and trajectory forecasting.

\section{Subquestions}

\subsection{Data and Observation Modeling}

The first subquestion concerns the data basis:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Which properties must synthetic and benchmark LiDAR datasets contain in order to evaluate tracking and trajectory prediction of small non-cooperative drones realistically?}
\end{quote}

This question addresses the fact that suitable public LiDAR datasets for small airborne drone encounters are limited. Existing large-scale datasets such as KITTI, nuScenes, and Waymo Open Dataset are invaluable for autonomous-driving research, but they do not represent the helicopter-oriented drone encounter problem directly \cite{geiger2012kitti,caesar2020nuscenes,sun2020waymo}. Therefore, synthetic data generation and carefully designed benchmark scenarios are necessary. However, synthetic data must be constructed in a way that avoids overly idealized conditions and accounts for the simulation-to-reality gap \cite{dieter2023simulationRealityGap,manduhu2023airborneSenseDetect}.

This subquestion investigates the role of sensor range, point sparsity, vertical field of view, measurement noise, drone size, drone reflectivity, relative motion, altitude variation, and multi-drone scenarios. In particular, it asks how benchmark data should be structured so that tracking and prediction methods are evaluated on unseen trajectories rather than temporally adjacent frames from the same recording.

\subsection{Tracking and State Estimation}

The second subquestion concerns tracking:

\begin{quote}
\textit{How can noisy and intermittent LiDAR detections of small drones be associated over time to estimate stable position and velocity tracks?}
\end{quote}

This question focuses on the transition from object detection to object state estimation. The detector provides 3D bounding boxes and confidence scores, but it does not by itself provide persistent identities, velocity estimates, or robustness against missed detections. Tracking-by-detection methods address this by combining motion prediction, detection-to-track association, and recursive state update. Classical Kalman filtering provides a principled framework for estimating object state under noisy measurements \cite{kalman1960newApproach}. Assignment methods such as the Hungarian algorithm provide a basis for matching detections to tracks in multi-object scenarios \cite{kuhn1955hungarian}. Practical tracking systems such as SORT and AB3DMOT demonstrate that simple motion models can be effective when combined with reliable detections and appropriate association logic \cite{bewley2016sort,weng2020ab3dmot}.

For sparse airborne LiDAR drone tracking, this subquestion investigates how such methods must be adapted to small objects, free 3D motion, intermittent detections, and altitude variation. It also asks how detection confidence, point count, and estimated measurement uncertainty can be incorporated into the tracking process.

\subsection{Short-Horizon Trajectory Prediction}

The third subquestion concerns prediction:

\begin{quote}
\textit{How can short-horizon future positions of non-cooperative drones be predicted from LiDAR-based tracks, and how should prediction quality be evaluated for a detect-and-avoid context?}
\end{quote}

This question extends tracking into future motion forecasting. Simple models such as constant-position, constant-velocity, and Kalman-based prediction provide transparent baselines. More complex learning-based models may capture nonlinear drone motion and maneuver patterns, but they require representative trajectory data and must remain robust to imperfect perception input. Existing work on non-cooperative UAV trajectory prediction and general multi-agent trajectory forecasting provides a foundation, but these methods are often not directly designed for sparse LiDAR-derived tracks \cite{xie2022noncooperativeUAVPrediction,tan2023flyingVehicleTrajectory,salzmann2020trajectronpp}.

The subquestion therefore investigates both model-based and learning-based predictors. It further asks how prediction performance should be measured. Standard forecasting metrics such as average displacement error and final displacement error are useful, but a helicopter detect-and-avoid application also requires attention to vertical error, missed detections, track continuity, prediction uncertainty, and conflict-relevant quantities such as future separation.

\section{Scope and Delimitation}

The dissertation focuses on perception, tracking, and short-horizon trajectory prediction. It does not aim to develop a complete helicopter flight-control or avoidance system. Instead, the output of the proposed pipeline is intended to provide safety-relevant intruder state estimates that could later be used by a detect-and-avoid or model-predictive-control module. The emphasis is therefore on the reliability of the perception and prediction layer rather than on the final avoidance maneuver.

The work also does not focus solely on improving generic 3D object detection performance. Detection quality remains important, but the dissertation evaluates whether detections can support stable tracks and future trajectory estimates. Consequently, the evaluation will include detection metrics, tracking metrics, prediction metrics, and scenario-level quantities relevant to safety-oriented perception.
